\title{Time Is the Baseline to Beat:\\Lead Time over a Timer as the Test for VLA Failure Monitors}

\author{Author Names Omitted for Anonymous Review. Paper-ID [add your ID here]}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Runtime failure monitors for vision-language-action (VLA) policies are often evaluated under
success-terminated, time-limited protocols, where a failed episode is one that reaches its time
limit. There, elapsed time is a strong failure signal: on RoboCasa365, a timer calibrated at a
5\% false-positive rate (FPR) flags every failure, and a model that sees only elapsed time
separates failures as well as one that reads the policy. Without a duration-only comparator,
even timeliness metrics cannot show what a monitor adds. We measure how much earlier than a
per-task timer a monitor alarms at the same target FPR, and fuse the two by an OR rule whose
split-conformal calibration bounds the FPR for a path fixed independently of the calibration
successes. On 760 test rollouts of one VLA on 38 tasks, at matched false alarms, three-seed
ensembles of the SAFE failure score and of the sequence detector Hide-and-Seek warn earlier than
the timer by 0.015 and 0.056 of the time limit, neither clearly above zero (0.030 and 0.071 at
the same target). Input ablations that see only elapsed time or another rollout's features add
none at matched false alarms. On unseen tasks, where no
per-task timer exists, Hide-and-Seek still adds 0.051 over a global timer at matched false alarms
(0.127 at the same target), SAFE about as much as random noise. The ordering holds on recorded rollouts of a second VLA, and on FIPER's benchmark
FIPER does warn earlier than a timer. Stage~2 tests the second VLA on new rollouts and whether earlier alarms
raise success through retry.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies~\cite{kim2024openvla,black2025pi0} fail often enough that
a deployed robot must notice when an attempt is going wrong. Runtime monitors map the policy's
hidden state to a failure score~\cite{gu2025safe}, or score how unusual its embeddings or actions
are relative to successful rollouts~\cite{xu2025faildetect,romer2025fiper}. They are compared
with each other on ROC-AUC, balanced accuracy and, increasingly,
timeliness~\cite{romer2025fiper,park2026hideseek}, but rarely with what the robot knows for free:
how long the attempt has been running.

Under success-terminated, time-limited protocols such as those of RoboCasa365~\cite{robocasa365}
and LIBERO~\cite{liu2023libero}, an episode ends at the first success or at a fixed time limit,
so a failure is, by construction, an episode that ran out of time. In RoboCasa365 every one of
our failed rollouts ran to its limit, and on our validation set a timer at 90\% of the limit
flags all of them at 4.7\% FPR. Elapsed time also explains separation: Hide-and-Seek's LSTM
trained on zero inputs, which sees only elapsed time, matches the ROC-AUC of the same LSTM
trained on the policy's features (0.888 against 0.878). On such benchmarks, a monitor can add only one thing over a timer: an earlier
alarm at the same false-alarm rate (Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative}), whose value Stage~2 tests by
acting on it.

We therefore evaluate monitors by their \emph{warning lead} over a per-task timer that alarms
once an attempt has run longer than the task's successful attempts usually take. We
hypothesize that the policy's internal state carries warning that elapsed time does not: fused
with this timer, a learned failure score should raise the mean lead at the same FPR, and the
gain should vanish when the score cannot see the rollout.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A duration-only comparator for timeliness metrics:} warning lead over a timer at
  matched FPR, on known and unseen tasks, with input ablations that separate rollout-specific
  evidence from learned elapsed time (Sec.~\ref{sec:setting}, \ref{sec:method}).
  \item \textbf{Time-aware fusion:} an OR rule that fuses any monitor with a timer, whose
  split-conformal calibration bounds the FPR in finite samples for a path fixed independently of
  the calibration successes (Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr}).
  \item \textbf{An evaluation on 38 RoboCasa365 tasks.} As three-seed ensembles at matched false
  alarms, SAFE adds 0.015 of the time limit over the per-task timer and
  Hide-and-Seek~\cite{park2026hideseek} 0.056, neither clearly above zero; input ablations
  suggest that Hide-and-Seek's warning develops during the rollout (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}). On
  XR-1's unseen tasks, Hide-and-Seek keeps the largest gain; the ordering holds on a second VLA,
  and on FIPER's benchmark FIPER does warn earlier than a timer.
\end{itemize}
Most failures we annotated had reached no irreversible state by the limit, which is why lead,
not recall, is the quantity to report.

\section{Problem Setup}
\label{sec:setting}

\textbf{Time.} The simulator runs \emph{control steps} at RoboCasa's default 20\,Hz. The policy
executes each 16-step action chunk in full, so \emph{policy inferences} $t = 1, 2, \dots$
(0.8\,s each) are our clock, and a monitor decides only at an inference. Task $k$'s limit of
$N_k$ control steps is $H_k = \lceil N_k/16 \rceil$ inferences (29 to 272, or 22.5 to 217.5\,s).
Every fraction of time ($t/H_k$, $c$, $\ell$) is a fraction of $H_k$.

\textbf{Monitoring.} A monitor watches a rollout of a known task $k$ and decides at each
inference $t$ whether to alarm, using only the policy features so far, $t$, the task and $H_k$.
The first alarm is at $T$ (in inferences). FPR is the fraction of successful rollouts with any
alarm. A rollout of length $L$ ends at success or at $H_k$. For a failure ($L = H_k$) the
\emph{warning lead} is $\ell = (L - T)/H_k$, one minus the normalized detection time
of~\cite{romer2025fiper}, with $\ell = 0$ for a missed failure; an alarm at the final inference
counts with $\ell = 0$. The mean lead $\bar\ell$ over failures combines recall and earliness.
Detectors are compared at thresholds set on a calibration set for FPR $\le \alpha = 0.05$ (the
\emph{same target}), and, since the realized test FPRs differ, also at \emph{matched} false
alarms: the comparator timer is set to the fused detector's number of test false alarms.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Monitor scores}

\textbf{Policy score.} At each inference we take the final-layer action-token hidden state of
the policy's action head (1,024 dimensions). The SAFE MLP~\cite{gu2025safe} (or, as a variant, SAFE's
LSTM head) maps the features so far to a failure score $u^{(j)}_t$ for each of three training
seeds $j$; as in SAFE, the MLP's score sums its per-inference outputs.\verifynote{SAFE-MLP form inferred
from train\_seen\_tasks default loss-mode official (pinned SAFE cumsum); confirm in the XR-1 and
RLDX-1 final-refit configs} With per-task location $m^{(j)}_k$ and
scale $\sigma^{(j)}_k$ estimated on training rollouts, the policy score is
\begin{equation}
  s_t = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{j=1}^{3} \frac{\max_{t' \le t} u^{(j)}_{t'} - m^{(j)}_k}{\sigma^{(j)}_k}.
\end{equation}
The running maximum makes $s_t$ monotone, so it does not change once a threshold is crossed.
On unseen tasks, $m^{(j)}$ and $\sigma^{(j)}$ are pooled over training tasks.
Success-only alternatives (Mahalanobis~\cite{lee2018mahalanobis}, kNN~\cite{sun2022knn},
PCA-KMeans~\cite{liu2024siriusfleet} and RND~\cite{burda2019rnd,he2024rediffuser}) are fitted on
training successes and aggregated the same way.

\textbf{Sequence score and input ablations.} Hide-and-Seek~\cite{park2026hideseek} trains an LSTM
with rollout labels only (in our implementation, on a 128-dimensional PCA of the same hidden
state): a margin loss pushes
each failure's peak score above every success's, and an onset loss acts within failures. A
recurrent model can learn how long each task takes, so we retrain it unchanged on ablated inputs:
\emph{zero input}, so it sees only elapsed time; \emph{shuffle}, a random same-task rollout's
features at the same inference, so it also sees the task's typical trajectory; and \emph{first
step}, the rollout's own first-inference features, repeated, so it also sees the initial scene.
Unlike the original
methods, which threshold with time-varying conformal bands~\cite{gu2025safe,park2026hideseek}, we
threshold every score at a constant level on its running maximum (Sec.~\ref{sec:guarantee}).

\subsection{Time baselines}

The \emph{global timer} G alarms once $t \ge c\,H_k$, with one fraction $c \in (0, 1]$ for all
tasks. The \emph{per-task timer} P uses $c_k = \min(1, \rho_k + \delta)$, where $\rho_k$ is the
95th percentile of task $k$'s training success durations (as fractions of $H_k$) and the shared
margin $\delta$ is set by Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr}. P needs successful rollouts of each task,
but no failures and no policy features.

\subsection{OR-fusion}

\input{figures/fusion}
The fused monitor (Fig.~\ref{fig:fusion}) alarms at
\begin{equation}
  T = \min\{\, t : s_t > \tau_s \ \text{or}\ t \ge c_k H_k \,\},
\end{equation}
with $c_k = c$ for G. Neither signal is restricted in time: the score may alarm early, and,
once calibration certifies a usable point (Sec.~\ref{sec:guarantee}), the timer alarms on every
failure by $c_k H_k$. Any lead
gain over the timer comes from failures on which the score crosses first.

\subsection{Calibration with a finite-sample FPR guarantee}
\label{sec:guarantee}

A successful rollout $i$ of task $k_i$ and length $L_i$ (in inferences) raises a false alarm
under $(\tau_s, c_1, \dots, c_K)$ if and only if $M_i = \max_{t \le L_i} s_t > \tau_s$ or
$d_i = L_i/H_{k_i} \ge c_{k_i}$. Fix in advance a finite grid $\Lambda$ and a path
$\gamma(\lambda) = (\tau_s(\lambda), c_1(\lambda), \dots, c_K(\lambda))$, $\lambda \in \Lambda$,
with every coordinate nondecreasing and $c_k \le 1$, so that a larger $\lambda$ is more
conservative and the alarm sets are nested. For each calibration success define
\begin{equation}
  V_i = \min\{\lambda \in \Lambda : M_i \le \tau_s(\lambda) \ \text{and}\ d_i < c_{k_i}(\lambda)\},
\end{equation}
the least conservative point on the path at which rollout $i$ stays silent, and $V_i = \infty$
if none is (e.g.\ a success lasting the whole limit).
Because the alarm sets are nested, rollout $i$ is silent at $\lambda \in \Lambda$ exactly when
$\lambda \ge V_i$.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:fpr}
Let $n$ calibration successes and a new successful rollout be exchangeable, and let the path
$\gamma$ not depend on the calibration successes. Set $\hat\lambda = V_{(r)}$, the $r$-th smallest
$V_i$ with $r = \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil$, or $\hat\lambda = \infty$ if $r > n$. If
$\hat\lambda = \infty$, the calibration rule cannot certify a usable point at the target and the
monitor is disabled: it raises no alarm. Otherwise it runs at $\gamma(\hat\lambda)$. Then, over
calibration draws, $\Pr(\text{monitor enabled and false alarm on the new success}) \le \alpha$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{IEEEproof}
An enabled monitor alarms on the new success if and only if $V_{n+1} > \hat\lambda$, and a
disabled one ($\hat\lambda = \infty$) never does, so the event is $\{V_{n+1} > V_{(r)}\}$ with
$V_{(r)} = \infty$ for $r > n$. By exchangeability, the rank of $V_{n+1}$ among
$V_1, \dots, V_{n+1}$ is uniform, so $\Pr(V_{n+1} > V_{(r)}) \le
1 - r/(n+1) \le \alpha$: the split-conformal quantile lemma~\cite{angelopoulos2021gentle}.
\end{IEEEproof}

The path may use any data except calibration successes, such as development rollouts or
calibration \emph{failures}. The guarantee is per trajectory and marginal over tasks and
calibration draws, not a cap on a finite test set or on each task (on the test set, one task
had 4 of 10 successes alarmed); a per-task version needs 19 calibration successes per task.

\textbf{Adaptive and fixed paths.} Our Stage~1 results use \emph{adaptive-path calibration}:
every detector fused with P uses the path $\delta = \lambda$, $\tau_s = a + b\lambda$, with
$(a, b)$ the grid point of largest mean lead on calibration failures, each point placed at its own
$\hat\lambda$. This choice also uses the calibration successes, so Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr}
bounds each candidate path, not the chosen one; over 200 re-splits of the successes, the choice
raised the expected test FPR from 0.047 (P) to about 0.055. A \emph{fixed path}, chosen
independently of the calibration successes, restores the bound: chosen on the validation set,
it gives Hide-and-Seek + P 0.072 at 0.061 FPR (0.057 matched, uncertain) and SAFE + P 0.007
(0.039 matched: its 16 false alarms against P's 21 make matching tighten P). Stage~2 uses fixed paths
only (Sec.~\ref{sec:plan}).

\section{Hypotheses}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

\textbf{Early warning (H1).} At a 5\% calibration target, OR-fusing a policy-internal score
with the per-task timer P increases the mean warning lead over P alone, also at matched false alarms.

\textbf{Policy evidence (H2).} At matched FPR, policy scores fused with P lead more than scores
without rollout-specific information: random noise, or features or scores resampled from other
rollouts of the task.

\textbf{Second policy (H3).} On a second VLA, RLDX-1~\cite{kim2026rldx}, SAFE-LSTM + P and
Hide-and-Seek + P also lead P, including at matched false alarms.

\textbf{Value of earlier warning (H4).} Within a budget of $1.25\,H_k$, resetting and retrying
when Hide-and-Seek + P alarms yields a higher success rate than resetting when P alarms.

Sec.~\ref{sec:results} tests H1 and H2; Stage~2 addresses H3 and H4 (Sec.~\ref{sec:plan}).

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Policy and tasks.} We use the released Xiaomi-Robotics-1 (XR-1) RoboCasa365
checkpoint~\cite{xiaomi2026robotics} on the 38 official RoboCasa365
tasks~\cite{nasiriany2024robocasa,robocasa365} whose published success lies between 5 and 45 of
50 (10 atomic and 28 composite tasks).

\textbf{Data.} Disjoint sets of rollouts serve one purpose each. A \emph{training set} (1,824
rollouts) fits every learned detector and P's $\rho_k$. A \emph{validation set} (380 successes
and 380 failures, 10 per class per task), with its own, smaller calibration set (3 successes and 3
failures per task), was used to develop the timers and the fused detectors. A \emph{test set} of
the same size, with new seeds, and a new calibration set of 10 successes and 10 failures per
task served the tests of H1 and H2.

\textbf{Unseen tasks.} Each task is held out once (4 folds): monitors are retrained and
thresholds calibrated without it, with OOD references and score normalization pooled over the
other tasks. P needs the task's own successes, so the comparator is G. (Hide-and-Seek's
evaluation~\cite{park2026hideseek} uses random 7/3 task splits instead.)

\textbf{Baselines.} We compare three families (Table~\ref{tab:raw}).
\emph{OOD detectors}, fitted on successful rollouts only: Mahalanobis
distance~\cite{lee2018mahalanobis}, cosine k-NN~\cite{sun2022knn},
PCA-KMeans~\cite{liu2024siriusfleet} and RND~\cite{he2024rediffuser}. \emph{Failure
classifiers}, trained with rollout labels: SAFE-MLP and SAFE-LSTM~\cite{gu2025safe} and
Hide-and-Seek~\cite{park2026hideseek}. \emph{Elapsed
time}, with no policy access: G and P. Token-uncertainty baselines need discrete action tokens
and STAC~\cite{agia2024sentinel} overlapping chunks, which XR-1 does not produce. ActProbe~\cite{huang2026actprobe},
reduced to its magnitude feature, never alarms on unseen tasks, so we omit it.

\textbf{Metrics.} Following~\cite{romer2025fiper,park2026hideseek}, at thresholds calibrated for
FPR $\le 0.05$ we report test FPR, balanced accuracy (bACC; equal to weighted accuracy on our
balanced sets), time-weighted accuracy $\mathrm{TWA} = \frac{1}{2}(\bar\ell + 1 -
\mathrm{FPR})$ and detection time $\mathrm{DT} = 1 - \bar\ell$, a miss counting as 1 (FIPER averages DT
over detected failures only). As
in~\cite{park2026hideseek}, each learned detector is trained with 3 random seeds, and tables give
the mean $\pm$ std of single models across these training seeds. For H1 and H2 we use
\emph{three-seed ensembles}, which average the 3 models' scores (Table~\ref{tab:h1}); the input
ablations are ensembled the same way. Matching sets P's margin on the test set itself, which
favors the timer. For
the two central matched gains, 95\% bootstrap CIs resample the test set's tasks, then failures
within each task (2,000 resamples, paired across methods). They are conditional on the fitted
thresholds and the matched timer, so they exclude calibration and matching uncertainty.

\subsection{Results on XR-1}

\textbf{Standalone detection.} On their own (Table~\ref{tab:raw}), learned detectors catch at most 69\% of seen-task failures
at 5\% FPR; the two timers, which never read the policy, catch all of them (bACC 0.97 and 0.99,
against at most 0.82), and P (DT 0.775) alarms earlier than every learned detector except
Hide-and-Seek (0.754). On unseen tasks, Hide-and-Seek and SAFE-LSTM exceed the global timer's TWA.

\textbf{SAFE and Hide-and-Seek (H1, H2).} Fused with P, the SAFE and Hide-and-Seek ensembles
warn 0.015 (95\% CI [$-0.007$, 0.041]) and 0.056 ([$-0.014$, 0.120]) of the time limit earlier
than P at matched false alarms, and 0.030 and 0.071 at the same target, where random and
within-task resampled scores gain at most 0.009 and 0.012 (Table~\ref{tab:h1};
Fig.~\ref{fig:pertask}b for other targets). Hide-and-Seek's advantage over SAFE (0.041) is
uncertain. A single Hide-and-Seek
model gains only $0.039 \pm 0.006$ (Table~\ref{tab:lead}); why averaging three nearly doubles
this is open, and Stage~2 reports gains by ensemble size.

\textbf{Where the gain comes from.} SAFE-MLP, Hide-and-Seek and the four OOD scores all gain
more on the 10 atomic tasks (0.045
to 0.160) than on the 28 composite ones (0.008 to 0.039; Fig.~\ref{fig:pertask}a). Hide-and-Seek
+ P alarms later than P on 16 tasks, where its looser timer margin spends the false-alarm budget
on the score, and five tasks carry 78\% of its gain. In seconds, Hide-and-Seek + P warns
2.6\,s earlier than P on average (matched: 5.0\,s on atomic, 0\,s on composite tasks).

\textbf{Stronger comparators and nulls.} A timer whose per-task margins maximize mean lead under
a pooled false-alarm budget, chosen as adaptively as the fused path, does not beat P at matched
false alarms ($-0.008$); Hide-and-Seek + P gains 0.063 over it. At matched false alarms, both
scores beat all 100 fusions of noise and of their own scores resampled within the task (mean
$-0.007$ to $-0.011$). With the methods' own time-varying
bands~\cite{gu2025safe,park2026hideseek}, fused matched gains are 0.068 (Hide-and-Seek) and
0.006 (SAFE). Matched gains stay uncertain when calibration is also resampled.

\textbf{Is the extra warning learned elapsed time?} A recurrent detector could learn task
durations, and the zero-input ablation separates failures as well as Hide-and-Seek (Sec.~I).
Yet no ablation gains over P at matched false alarms (Table~\ref{tab:h1}): the warning appears to
develop during the rollout and is neither elapsed time nor the task's typical trajectory.

\subsection{Transfer and irreversibility}
\textbf{Unseen tasks (Tables~\ref{tab:raw} and~\ref{tab:lead}).} With no rollout of the task, as in SAFE's setting~\cite{gu2025safe},
Hide-and-Seek keeps the largest gain over G on XR-1: 0.127 as an ensemble (zero-input
ablation: 0.033), 0.11 more than SAFE. SAFE's ensemble (0.017) gains no more than random
scores (up to 0.019), and $-0.079$ matched (Hide-and-Seek: 0.051). Once 1 to 5 successes of the new task exist, a
per-task timer can be built, and Hide-and-Seek still adds 0.088 to 0.099 over it (SAFE about
0.01). SAFE's LSTM head carries over better than its MLP, with half of Hide-and-Seek's gain
(Table~\ref{tab:lead}).

\textbf{Second policy.} On recorded RLDX-1 rollouts~\cite{kim2026rldx} (20 tasks;
Table~\ref{tab:lead}, bottom), all failures again ran to the limit. As ensembles, SAFE-LSTM + P
gains 0.034 and Hide-and-Seek + P 0.083 (0.052 matched), both on atomic tasks only. On unseen tasks,
Hide-and-Seek again gains most over G ($0.196 \pm 0.013$; ablations $\le 0.038$); SAFE-LSTM and
the OOD detectors gain 0.099 to 0.115 over this late timer (lead 0.026).

\textbf{FIPER's benchmark.} In FIPER's released rollouts~\cite{romer2025fiper} (five tasks, two
on a real robot), length alone gives ROC-AUC 0.92--1.00 in four tasks. In FIPER's code (results
reproduced), the timer has the best balanced accuracy under FIPER's selection rule (0.81 against
0.78) at 0.09 FPR, against FIPER's 0.35; at FIPER's own FPR (0.06 to 0.56 by task), FIPER warns
0.146 of the episode earlier: the test credits early warning wherever it occurs.

\textbf{When do failures become irreversible?} An annotator blind to alarms marked the point of
no return (the first state from which the task could not be completed without outside help) on
48 test failures: 38 (79\%), mostly slow or near-successful, reached none before the limit (a
second annotator agreed on 11 of 12). On the other 10, P alarmed before it on none, SAFE + P and
Hide-and-Seek + P on two each.

\section{Stage 2: Planned Experiments}
\label{sec:plan}

% Design and sizing: docs/stage2_sizing.md (main) and R9/R10 in docs/xr1_review_analyses_plan.md
% (branch claude/review-analyses; runs stage2_frozen_sizing_20261029_190042, e2_freeze_20261029_205743).
% Author decisions 2026-10-29: E1 rule = 97.5% CIs (same target and matched), binomial FPR rule,
% frozen null test; E2 = branching design at 1,000 scenes, estimated with CIs.
\textbf{Second policy (E1).} We test H3 on new RLDX-1 rollouts: fresh calibration and test sets
of 20 successes and 20 failures for each of its 20 tasks (about 2,500 rollouts). The detectors
(SAFE-LSTM, as SAFE's MLP head adds no lead on the recorded rollouts, and Hide-and-Seek), $H_k$,
$\rho_k$ and each fused path are fixed on the existing rollouts, so only $\hat\lambda$ is set on
new successes and Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr} applies. A gain over P counts as early warning if
its 97.5\% CI (Bonferroni over two detectors) is above zero at the same target and at matched
false alarms, it beats 38 of 39 random and resampled fusions, and test false alarms stay within
an exact binomial bound (at most 39 of 400); simulations with every path fixed give power of at
least 0.94 and no false positives under either null.

\textbf{Reset and retry (E2).} We evaluate H4 in closed loop on 1,000 RLDX-1 scenes (50 per
task) with a shared budget of $1.25\,H_k$. Each scene runs once without reset to $1.25\,H_k$
(the no-reset outcome); each monitor's arm branches from this run at its first alarm, restores
the initial simulator state and retries with the same policy seed, with latency charged to the
budget. We report the paired success-rate difference between Hide-and-Seek + P and P resets with
a 95\% CI, plus reset against no reset and unnecessary resets. Simulations from the
recorded rollouts put it at about $+0.01$ (half-width 0.02), so E2 estimates H4's effect rather
than testing it; in a pilot, only 4\% of RLDX-1 failures (18 of 444) succeeded when continued to
$1.25\,H_k$.

\textbf{Further analyses (E3).} Action-only and multi-sample monitors, and lead from the
point of no return.

\section{Conclusion and Limitations}

At matched false alarms, policy monitors add an uncertain 0.015 to 0.056 of the time limit over
a per-task timer, so early warning (H1) is not yet established; Stage~2 tests it further.

\textbf{Limitations.} Alarms were recorded in simulation (free resets), not acted on; lead
runs to the time limit, not to irreversibility; RLDX-1 rollouts are recorded. Hide-and-Seek
was tested on a test set already used for SAFE, and analyses beyond H1 and H2 followed scoring.
Monitors assume a known task, a time limit and an automatic success check.
