\begin{abstract}
Runtime failure monitors for vision-language-action (VLA) policies are often evaluated under
success-terminated, time-limited protocols, where a failed episode is one that reaches its time
limit. There, elapsed time is a strong failure signal: on RoboCasa365, a timer calibrated at a
5\% false-positive rate (FPR) flags every failure, and a model that sees only elapsed time
separates failures as well as one that reads the policy. Without a duration-only comparator,
even timeliness metrics cannot show what a monitor adds. We measure how much earlier than a
per-task timer a monitor alarms at the same target FPR, and fuse the two by an OR rule whose
split-conformal margin bounds the FPR of a path fixed in advance. In a preregistered test on 760 fresh
rollouts of one VLA on 38 tasks, the SAFE failure score adds 0.030 of the time limit to the timer
(95\% CI [0.007, 0.093]), short of the 0.05 we required, while the sequence detector
Hide-and-Seek adds 0.071 ([0.022, 0.157]) with 3 seeds averaged and $0.039 \pm 0.006$ as a single
model; at matched false alarms they shrink to 0.015 and 0.056, with CIs including zero. Controls that see only elapsed time or another rollout's features
add at most 0.012. On
unseen tasks, Hide-and-Seek still adds 0.127 over a global timer, SAFE about as much as noise.
The ordering replicates offline on a second VLA, and on FIPER's benchmark the test credits
FIPER. Stage~2 tests a second VLA prospectively and whether earlier alarms raise success
through retry.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies fail often enough that a deployed robot must notice when an
attempt is going wrong. Runtime monitors map the policy's hidden state to a failure
score~\cite{gu2025safe}, or score how unusual its embeddings or actions are relative to
successful rollouts~\cite{xu2025faildetect,romer2025fiper}. They are compared with each other on
ROC-AUC, balanced accuracy and, increasingly, timeliness~\cite{romer2025fiper,park2026hideseek},
but rarely with what the robot knows for free: how long the attempt has been running.

Under success-terminated, time-limited protocols such as RoboCasa365's, an episode ends at the
first success or at a fixed time limit, so a failure is, by construction, an episode that ran out
of time. In RoboCasa365
every one of our failed rollouts ran to its limit, and a timer at 90\% of the limit flags all of
them at 4.7\% FPR (Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative}). Elapsed time also explains separation: an LSTM
trained on zero inputs, which sees only elapsed time, matches the ROC-AUC of the same LSTM
trained on the policy's features (0.888 against 0.878). On such benchmarks, a monitor can add
only one thing over a timer: an earlier alarm at the same false-alarm rate, whose value
Stage~2 tests by acting on it.

We therefore evaluate monitors by their \emph{warning lead} over a per-task timer that alarms
once an attempt has run longer than the task's successful attempts usually take. We
hypothesize that the policy's internal state carries warning that elapsed time does not: fused
with this timer, a learned failure score should raise the mean lead by a meaningful margin at
the same FPR, and the gain should vanish when the score is replaced by one that cannot see the
rollout.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A duration-only comparator for timeliness metrics:} warning lead over a timer at
  matched FPR, on known and unseen tasks, with controls that separate rollout-specific evidence
  from learned elapsed time (Sec.~\ref{sec:setting}, \ref{sec:method}).
  \item \textbf{A calibrated OR-fusion} of a monitor with a timer, whose split-conformal rule
  bounds the FPR in finite samples for a calibration path fixed in advance
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr}).
  \item \textbf{A preregistered evaluation} on 38 RoboCasa365 tasks. SAFE adds 0.030 of the time
  limit over the per-task timer, below our 0.05 margin; Hide-and-Seek~\cite{park2026hideseek}
  adds 0.071, and controls show that its warning develops during the rollout
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}). On XR-1, only Hide-and-Seek's gain carries over to unseen tasks; the result
  replicates offline on a second VLA, and on FIPER's released benchmark the test credits FIPER.
\end{itemize}
Most failures we labeled had reached no irreversible state by the limit, which is why lead, not
recall, is the quantity to report.

\section{Problem Setup}
\label{sec:setting}

\textbf{Time.} The simulator runs \emph{control steps} at RoboCasa's default 20\,Hz. The policy
executes each 16-step action chunk in full, so \emph{policy inferences} $t = 1, 2, \dots$
(0.8\,s each) are our clock, and a monitor decides only at an inference. Task $k$'s limit of
$T_k$ control steps is $H_k = \lceil T_k/16 \rceil$ inferences (29 to 272, or 22.5 to 217.5\,s).
Every fraction of time ($t/H_k$, $c$, $\ell$) is a fraction of $H_k$.

\textbf{Monitoring.} A monitor watches a rollout of a known task $k$ and decides at each
inference $t$ whether to alarm, using only the policy features so far, $t$, the task and $H_k$.
The first alarm is at $T$ (in inferences). FPR is the fraction of successful rollouts with any
alarm. A rollout of length $L$ ends at success or at $H_k$. For a failure ($L = H_k$) the
\emph{warning lead} is $\ell = (L - T)/H_k$, one minus the normalized detection time
of~\cite{romer2025fiper}, with $\ell = 0$ for a missed failure; an alarm at the final inference
counts with $\ell = 0$. The mean lead $\bar\ell$ over failures combines recall and earliness.
Detectors are compared at thresholds set on a calibration set for FPR $\le \alpha = 0.05$.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Monitor scores}

\textbf{Policy score.} At each inference we take the final-layer action-token hidden state of
the policy's action head (1,024 dimensions). The SAFE MLP~\cite{gu2025safe} maps it to a failure
score $u^{(j)}_t$ for each of three training seeds $j$. With per-task location $m^{(j)}_k$ and
scale $\sigma^{(j)}_k$ estimated on training rollouts, the policy score is
\begin{equation}
  s_t = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{j=1}^{3} \frac{\max_{t' \le t} u^{(j)}_{t'} - m^{(j)}_k}{\sigma^{(j)}_k}.
\end{equation}
The running maximum makes $s_t$ monotone, so it does not change once a threshold is crossed.
On unseen tasks, $m^{(j)}$ and $\sigma^{(j)}$ are pooled over training tasks.
Success-only alternatives (Mahalanobis~\cite{lee2018mahalanobis}, kNN~\cite{sun2022knn},
PCA-KMeans~\cite{liu2024siriusfleet} and RND~\cite{burda2019rnd,he2024rediffuser}) are fitted on
training successes and aggregated the same way.

\textbf{Sequence score and controls.} Hide-and-Seek~\cite{park2026hideseek} trains an LSTM on a
128-dimensional PCA of the same hidden state with rollout labels only: a margin loss pushes each
failure's peak score above every success's, and an onset loss acts within failures. A recurrent
model can learn how long each task takes, so we retrain it unchanged on (i) zero inputs, so it
sees only elapsed time; (ii) a random same-task rollout's features at the same inference, so it
also sees the task's typical trajectory; and (iii) the rollout's own first-inference features,
repeated, so it also sees the episode's initial scene. ActProbe~\cite{huang2026actprobe}
reduces to chunk magnitude here, as our chunks never overlap.

\subsection{Time baselines}

The \emph{global timer} alarms once $t \ge c\,H_k$, with one fraction $c \in (0, 1]$ for all
tasks. The \emph{per-task timer} P uses $c_k = \min(1, \rho_k + \delta)$, where $\rho_k$ is the
95th percentile of task $k$'s training success durations (as fractions of $H_k$) and the shared
margin $\delta$ is set by Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr}. P needs successful rollouts of each task,
but no failures and no policy features. The
\emph{time risk} estimates $\Pr(\text{failure} \mid k, \text{still running at } t)$ from
training rollouts by an increasing isotonic regression in $t/H_k$. As every failure runs to
$H_k$, it is a task-specific timer, kept low by smoothing where training failures are few.

\subsection{OR-fusion}

The fused monitor alarms at
\begin{equation}
  T = \min\{\, t : s_t > \tau_s \ \text{or}\ t \ge c\,H_k \,\}.
\end{equation}
Neither signal is restricted in time: the score may alarm early, and, once calibration certifies
a usable point (Sec.~\ref{sec:guarantee}), the timer alarms on every failure by $c\,H_k$. Any lead gain over the timer comes from
failures on which the score crosses first.

\subsection{Calibration with a finite-sample FPR guarantee}
\label{sec:guarantee}

A successful rollout $i$ of length $L_i$ (in inferences) raises a false alarm under
$(\tau_s, c)$ if and only if $M_i = \max_{t \le L_i} s_t > \tau_s$ or $\rho_i = L_i/H_{k_i}
\ge c$, so its false alarm depends only on the pair $(M_i, \rho_i)$. Fix in advance a path
$\gamma(\lambda) = (\tau_s(\lambda), c(\lambda))$, $\lambda \ge 0$, with both coordinates
nondecreasing and $c \le 1$, so that a larger $\lambda$ is more conservative. For each
calibration success define
\begin{equation}
  V_i = \min\{\lambda : M_i \le \tau_s(\lambda) \ \text{and}\ \rho_i < c(\lambda)\},
\end{equation}
the least conservative point on the path at which rollout $i$ stays silent, and $V_i = \infty$
if none is: a success that lasts the whole limit ($\rho_i \ge 1$) alarms at every point.
Because the path is monotone, rollout $i$ is silent at $\lambda$ exactly when
$\lambda \ge V_i$.\footnote{$\lambda$ takes the finitely many values at which the timer (one
inference) or the score changes the alarm, so the minimum is attained.}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:fpr}
Let $n$ calibration successes and a new successful rollout be exchangeable, and let the path
$\gamma$ not depend on the calibration successes. Set $\hat\lambda = V_{(r)}$, the $r$-th smallest
$V_i$ with $r = \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil$, or $\hat\lambda = \infty$ if $r > n$. If
$\hat\lambda = \infty$, the calibration rule cannot certify a usable point at the target and the
monitor is disabled: it raises no alarm. Otherwise it runs at $\gamma(\hat\lambda)$. Then, over
calibration draws, $\Pr(\text{monitor enabled and false alarm on the new success}) \le \alpha$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{IEEEproof}
An enabled monitor alarms on the new success if and only if $V_{n+1} > \hat\lambda$, and a
disabled one ($\hat\lambda = \infty$) never does, so the event is $\{V_{n+1} > V_{(r)}\}$ with
$V_{(r)} = \infty$ for $r > n$. By exchangeability, the rank of $V_{n+1}$ among
$V_1, \dots, V_{n+1}$ is uniform (ties broken at random), so $\Pr(V_{n+1} > V_{(r)}) \le
1 - r/(n+1) \le \alpha$: the split-conformal quantile lemma~\cite{angelopoulos2021gentle}.
\end{IEEEproof}

The path may use any data except calibration successes, such as development rollouts or
calibration \emph{failures}. The guarantee is per trajectory and marginal over tasks and
calibration draws, not a cap on a finite test set or on each task (on the held-out set, one task
had 4 of 10 successes alarmed); a per-task version needs 19 calibration successes per task.

\textbf{Choosing the path.} Every detector fused with P, on all test sets, uses
Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr} with the path $\delta = \lambda$, $\tau_s = u + s\lambda$, where
$(u, s)$ is the grid point with the largest mean lead on calibration failures, each point placed
at its own $\hat\lambda$ from the calibration successes. The selected path thus depends on those
successes: Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr} bounds the FPR of each candidate path, not of the one
chosen among them, which is why our criteria also check the test FPR
(Sec.~\ref{sec:hypotheses}); over 200 re-splits of the successes, the selection raised the
expected test FPR from 0.047 (P) to about 0.055. A path chosen on other rollouts and frozen
restores the bound: with a \emph{frozen} path chosen on the validation set (exploratory),
Hide-and-Seek + P still gains 0.072 at 0.061 FPR and SAFE + P 0.007, but both decision CIs
include zero ([$-0.069$, 0.143], [$-0.222$, 0.067]). Other results use the registered
\emph{adaptive} path; E2 freezes paths (Sec.~\ref{sec:plan}).

\section{Hypotheses}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

\textbf{H1 (earlier warning).} At thresholds calibrated for FPR $\le 0.05$, OR-fusing a
policy-internal score with the per-task timer P, the strongest time-only monitor we know,
increases the mean warning lead over P alone. We count H1 as supported if the 95\% bootstrap CI
of the gain lies above zero, the gain is at least $0.05$ of the time limit, and the fused test
FPR is at most $0.075$ with a Wilson 95\% upper bound of at most $0.10$.

\textbf{H2 (the gain needs policy evidence).} Fusing P with scores that carry no policy evidence
(Gaussian noise, or the policy score resampled within task) gains less than $0.05$ over P.

\textbf{H3 (second policy).} On RLDX-1's 20 tasks, both SAFE + P and Hide-and-Seek + P increase
the mean lead over P: each 97.5\% CI (Bonferroni over the two) lies above zero, with the
criterion of Sec.~\ref{sec:plan}. No margin is required, and the claim is conditional on these
20 tasks.

\textbf{H4 (utility of the extra warning).} Within a budget of $1.25\,H_k$, a reset and retry
triggered by Hide-and-Seek + P yields a higher success rate than the same reset triggered by P.

We fixed H1, H2, P, the metric and the criteria before computing any timer or lead on a fresh
held-out test set, and preregistered the same test for Hide-and-Seek before scoring it there.
Stage~2 tests H3 and estimates H4 (Sec.~\ref{sec:plan}). From the validation set we expected
H1 to fail for SAFE (gain 0.007) and to hold for Hide-and-Seek (0.083 at matched FPR).

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Policy and tasks.} We use the released Xiaomi-Robotics-1 (XR-1) RoboCasa365
checkpoint~\cite{xiaomi2026robotics} on the 38 official RoboCasa365
tasks~\cite{nasiriany2024robocasa,robocasa365} whose published success lies between 5 and 45 of
50 (10 atomic and 28 composite tasks).

\textbf{Data.} Disjoint sets of rollouts serve one purpose each. A \emph{training set} (1,824
rollouts) fits every learned detector, the time risk and P's $\rho_k$. A \emph{validation set}
(380 successes and 380 failures, 10 per class per task), with its own calibration set (3
successes and 3 failures per task), was used to develop the timers and the fused detectors.
A fresh \emph{held-out test set} of the same size, with new seeds, and a new
calibration set of 380 successes and 380 failures served the preregistered tests.

\textbf{Baselines.} We compare three families (Table~\ref{tab:raw}).
\emph{OOD detectors}, fitted on successful rollouts only: Mahalanobis
distance~\cite{lee2018mahalanobis}, cosine k-NN~\cite{sun2022knn},
PCA-KMeans~\cite{liu2024siriusfleet} and RND~\cite{he2024rediffuser}. \emph{Failure
classifiers}, trained with rollout labels: SAFE-MLP~\cite{gu2025safe},
ActProbe~\cite{huang2026actprobe} and Hide-and-Seek~\cite{park2026hideseek}. \emph{Elapsed
time}, with no policy access: the time risk, the global timer and P. Token-uncertainty baselines
need discrete action tokens and STAC~\cite{agia2024sentinel} overlapping chunks, which XR-1 does
not produce; multi-sample baselines are part of E4.

\textbf{Metrics.} Following~\cite{romer2025fiper,park2026hideseek}, at thresholds calibrated for
FPR $\le 0.05$ we report TPR, test FPR, balanced accuracy (bACC; weighted accuracy equals it on
our balanced sets), time-weighted accuracy $\mathrm{TWA} = \frac{1}{2}(\bar\ell + 1 -
\mathrm{FPR})$ and detection time $\mathrm{DT} = 1 - \bar\ell$, a miss counting as 1. As
in~\cite{park2026hideseek}, learned detectors are trained with 3 random seeds and we report the
mean and std of single models; our seeds change only training, not the data or
splits. The preregistered detectors instead average the 3
seeds' scores; their decision intervals are 95\% bootstrap CIs over tasks, then rollouts, that
also resample the calibration set and refit all thresholds (2,000 resamples). \emph{Matched} gains
compare with P loosened on the test set to the fused detector's false alarms, which
favors the timer.

\subsection{Standalone failure detection}

Evaluated on their own, as in prior work (Table~\ref{tab:raw}), the OOD detectors and failure
classifiers catch 36\% to 69\% of the failures on seen tasks at 5\% FPR. The two timers, which
never read the policy, catch all of them (bACC 0.97 and 0.99, against at most 0.82). The global
timer alarms last, but P (DT 0.775) alarms earlier than every learned detector except
Hide-and-Seek (0.754), which alone exceeds its TWA. On unseen tasks, Hide-and-Seek is the only
detector above the global timer's TWA, and ActProbe's thresholds almost never fire.

\subsection{Warning lead over the per-task timer}

\textbf{SAFE: a small but real gain, below the margin (H1 not supported, H2 supported).} On the
held-out set (Table~\ref{tab:lead}), SAFE + P leads P by 0.030 of the time limit (95\% CI [0.007,
0.093]). The CI lies above zero and the test
FPR (0.061, Wilson upper bound 0.089) meets the criterion, but the point gain is below the 0.05
margin; the CI contains 0.05, so the rule, not the interval, decides.
Noise and within-task resampled scores gain at most 0.009, so SAFE adds policy evidence, not
an artifact of fusion. P loosened to SAFE + P's FPR leaves SAFE 0.015 ([$-0.007$, 0.041]). All
five policy scores gain more on the 10 atomic tasks (0.045 to 0.081) than on the 28 composite
ones (0.008 to 0.027).

\textbf{Hide-and-Seek: an earlier warning (H1 and H2 supported).} Hide-and-Seek + P leads P by
0.071 ([0.022, 0.157]) at 0.061 FPR (Wilson bound 0.089), meeting every criterion; noise and resampled scores gain
at most 0.012. At matched false alarms it gains 0.056 ([$-0.014$, 0.120]), positive at every
target from 0.02 to 0.10 (SAFE: 0.013 on average). Its advantage over SAFE + P, 0.041
([$-0.011$, 0.103]), is not significant; as the bootstrap is skewed upward, the point gain
carries the decision. A single Hide-and-Seek model gains
only $0.039 \pm 0.006$ (Table~\ref{tab:lead}), so seed averaging is part of its warning. The
gain is uneven: on 16 of 38 tasks the fusion alarms later than P, as its looser margin
spends the false-alarm budget on the score, and five tasks carry 78\% of the gain.

\subsection{Is the extra warning learned elapsed time?}

A recurrent detector trained on rollouts that all fail at the limit could learn task durations.
The zero-input control matches Hide-and-Seek's validation ROC-AUC and fused recall
(0.974 vs.\ 0.976), yet adds 0.001 over P at matched FPR. On the held-out set, Hide-and-Seek
beats the first-inference, zero-input and shuffle controls by 0.070, 0.059 and 0.060 (CIs above
zero): its warning develops during the rollout and is neither elapsed time nor the task's
typical trajectory.

\subsection{When do failures become irreversible?}

One labeler, blind to alarms, marked the point of no return (the first state from which the task
could not be completed without outside help) on 48 held-out failures: 38 (79\%) reached no such state before the limit
(\emph{recoverable}), most often because the robot was slow or nearly succeeded; a second labeler agreed on 11 of 12 ($\kappa = 0.80$).
An early ``failure'' alarm thus mostly flags an attempt that is not yet lost. On all 10 decided failures, P alarmed
after the point of no return, and no detector flags more than 8\% of failures by half the limit.

\section{Conclusion and Limitations}

When failure means running out of time, a monitor must beat a timer at the same false-alarm
rate. Against a per-task timer at the same target FPR, SAFE added 0.030 of the time limit on a
preregistered held-out set and Hide-and-Seek 0.071 (0.015 and 0.056 at matched false alarms), a
warning that controls trace to the
rollout and that carries over to unseen tasks. Stage~2 tests both on a second policy and
estimates their value in closed-loop retry.

\textbf{Limitations.} Alarms were recorded, not acted on, in simulation, and lead is measured to
the time limit, not to an irreversible failure. RLDX-1 is evaluated only offline. The timers
and fusions came after the validation set was opened, the standalone, matched, per-task and
unseen-task analyses after the held-out set was, and
Hide-and-Seek, the better of two supervised baselines there, was tested on a held-out set
already used for SAFE. Monitors assume a known task and time limit; fixed-horizon data
need failure-time labels. Only 10 labeled failures were decided, and the FIPER lead
comparison is post hoc.
