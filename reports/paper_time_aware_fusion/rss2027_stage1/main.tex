\begin{abstract}
Runtime failure monitors for vision-language-action (VLA) policies are often evaluated under
success-terminated, time-limited protocols, where a failed episode is one that reaches its time
limit. There, elapsed time is a strong failure signal: on RoboCasa365, a timer calibrated at a
5\% false-positive rate (FPR) flags every failure, and a model that sees only elapsed time
separates failures as well as one that reads the policy. Without a duration-only comparator,
even timeliness metrics cannot show what a monitor adds. We measure how much earlier than a
per-task timer a monitor alarms at the same target FPR, and fuse the two by an OR rule whose
split-conformal calibration bounds the FPR for a path fixed independently of the calibration
successes. On 760 test rollouts of one VLA on 38 tasks, three-seed ensembles of the SAFE failure
score and of the sequence detector Hide-and-Seek add 0.030 and 0.071 of the time limit to the
timer; at matched false alarms they shrink to 0.015 and 0.056, neither clearly above zero. Input ablations that see
only elapsed time or another rollout's features add at most 0.012. On unseen tasks, where no
per-task timer exists, Hide-and-Seek still adds 0.127 over a global timer, SAFE about as much as
random noise. The ordering holds on recorded rollouts of a second VLA, and on FIPER's benchmark
the test credits FIPER. Stage~2 tests the second VLA on new rollouts and whether earlier alarms
raise success through retry.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies~\cite{kim2024openvla,black2025pi0} fail often enough that
a deployed robot must notice when an attempt is going wrong. Runtime monitors map the policy's
hidden state to a failure score~\cite{gu2025safe}, or score how unusual its embeddings or actions
are relative to successful rollouts~\cite{xu2025faildetect,romer2025fiper}. They are compared
with each other on ROC-AUC, balanced accuracy and, increasingly,
timeliness~\cite{romer2025fiper,park2026hideseek}, but rarely with what the robot knows for free:
how long the attempt has been running.

Under success-terminated, time-limited protocols such as those of RoboCasa365~\cite{robocasa365}
and LIBERO~\cite{liu2023libero}, an episode ends at the first success or at a fixed time limit,
so a failure is, by construction, an episode that ran out of time. In RoboCasa365 every one of
our failed rollouts ran to its limit, and a timer at 90\% of the limit flags all of them at
4.7\% FPR. Elapsed time also explains separation: an LSTM trained on zero inputs, which sees
only elapsed time, matches the ROC-AUC of the same LSTM trained on the policy's features (0.888
against 0.878). On such benchmarks, a monitor can add only one thing over a timer: an earlier
alarm at the same false-alarm rate (Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative}), whose value Stage~2 tests by
acting on it.

We therefore evaluate monitors by their \emph{warning lead} over a per-task timer that alarms
once an attempt has run longer than the task's successful attempts usually take. We
hypothesize that the policy's internal state carries warning that elapsed time does not: fused
with this timer, a learned failure score should raise the mean lead at the same FPR, and the
gain should vanish when the score cannot see the rollout.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A duration-only comparator for timeliness metrics:} warning lead over a timer at
  matched FPR, on known and unseen tasks, with input ablations that separate rollout-specific
  evidence from learned elapsed time (Sec.~\ref{sec:setting}, \ref{sec:method}).
  \item \textbf{Time-aware fusion:} an OR rule that fuses any monitor with a timer, whose
  split-conformal calibration bounds the FPR in finite samples for a path fixed independently of
  the calibration successes (Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr}).
  \item \textbf{An evaluation on 38 RoboCasa365 tasks.} As three-seed ensembles, SAFE adds 0.030 of the
  time limit over the per-task timer and Hide-and-Seek~\cite{park2026hideseek} 0.071, a warning that develops during the rollout
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}). On XR-1's unseen tasks, only Hide-and-Seek's gain carries over; the
  ordering holds on a second VLA, and on FIPER's benchmark the test credits FIPER.
\end{itemize}
Most failures we annotated had reached no irreversible state by the limit, which is why lead,
not recall, is the quantity to report.

\section{Problem Setup}
\label{sec:setting}

\textbf{Time.} The simulator runs \emph{control steps} at RoboCasa's default 20\,Hz. The policy
executes each 16-step action chunk in full, so \emph{policy inferences} $t = 1, 2, \dots$
(0.8\,s each) are our clock, and a monitor decides only at an inference. Task $k$'s limit of
$T_k$ control steps is $H_k = \lceil T_k/16 \rceil$ inferences (29 to 272, or 22.5 to 217.5\,s).
Every fraction of time ($t/H_k$, $c$, $\ell$) is a fraction of $H_k$.

\textbf{Monitoring.} A monitor watches a rollout of a known task $k$ and decides at each
inference $t$ whether to alarm, using only the policy features so far, $t$, the task and $H_k$.
The first alarm is at $T$ (in inferences). FPR is the fraction of successful rollouts with any
alarm. A rollout of length $L$ ends at success or at $H_k$. For a failure ($L = H_k$) the
\emph{warning lead} is $\ell = (L - T)/H_k$, one minus the normalized detection time
of~\cite{romer2025fiper}, with $\ell = 0$ for a missed failure; an alarm at the final inference
counts with $\ell = 0$. The mean lead $\bar\ell$ over failures combines recall and earliness.
Detectors are compared at thresholds set on a calibration set for FPR $\le \alpha = 0.05$.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Monitor scores}

\textbf{Policy score.} At each inference we take the final-layer action-token hidden state of
the policy's action head (1,024 dimensions). The SAFE MLP~\cite{gu2025safe} maps it to a failure
score $u^{(j)}_t$ for each of three training seeds $j$. With per-task location $m^{(j)}_k$ and
scale $\sigma^{(j)}_k$ estimated on training rollouts, the policy score is
\begin{equation}
  s_t = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{j=1}^{3} \frac{\max_{t' \le t} u^{(j)}_{t'} - m^{(j)}_k}{\sigma^{(j)}_k}.
\end{equation}
The running maximum makes $s_t$ monotone, so it does not change once a threshold is crossed.
On unseen tasks, $m^{(j)}$ and $\sigma^{(j)}$ are pooled over training tasks.
Success-only alternatives (Mahalanobis~\cite{lee2018mahalanobis}, kNN~\cite{sun2022knn},
PCA-KMeans~\cite{liu2024siriusfleet} and RND~\cite{burda2019rnd,he2024rediffuser}) are fitted on
training successes and aggregated the same way.

\textbf{Sequence score and input ablations.} Hide-and-Seek~\cite{park2026hideseek} trains an LSTM
on a 128-dimensional PCA of the same hidden state with rollout labels only: a margin loss pushes
each failure's peak score above every success's, and an onset loss acts within failures. A
recurrent model can learn how long each task takes, so we retrain it unchanged on ablated inputs:
\emph{zero input}, so it sees only elapsed time; \emph{shuffle}, a random same-task rollout's
features at the same inference, so it also sees the task's typical trajectory; and \emph{first
step}, the rollout's own first-inference features, repeated, so it also sees the initial scene.
ActProbe~\cite{huang2026actprobe} reduces to chunk magnitude here, as our chunks never overlap.

\subsection{Time baselines}

The \emph{global timer} G alarms once $t \ge c\,H_k$, with one fraction $c \in (0, 1]$ for all
tasks. The \emph{per-task timer} P uses $c_k = \min(1, \rho_k + \delta)$, where $\rho_k$ is the
95th percentile of task $k$'s training success durations (as fractions of $H_k$) and the shared
margin $\delta$ is set by Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr}. P needs successful rollouts of each task,
but no failures and no policy features.

\subsection{OR-fusion}

\input{figures/fusion}
The fused monitor (Fig.~\ref{fig:fusion}) alarms at
\begin{equation}
  T = \min\{\, t : s_t > \tau_s \ \text{or}\ t \ge c\,H_k \,\}.
\end{equation}
Neither signal is restricted in time: the score may alarm early, and, once calibration certifies
a usable point (Sec.~\ref{sec:guarantee}), the timer alarms on every failure by $c\,H_k$. Any lead
gain over the timer comes from failures on which the score crosses first.

\subsection{Calibration with a finite-sample FPR guarantee}
\label{sec:guarantee}

A successful rollout $i$ of length $L_i$ (in inferences) raises a false alarm under
$(\tau_s, c)$ if and only if $M_i = \max_{t \le L_i} s_t > \tau_s$ or $\rho_i = L_i/H_{k_i}
\ge c$, so its false alarm depends only on the pair $(M_i, \rho_i)$. Fix in advance a path
$\gamma(\lambda) = (\tau_s(\lambda), c(\lambda))$, $\lambda \ge 0$, with both coordinates
nondecreasing and $c \le 1$, so that a larger $\lambda$ is more conservative. For each
calibration success define
\begin{equation}
  V_i = \min\{\lambda : M_i \le \tau_s(\lambda) \ \text{and}\ \rho_i < c(\lambda)\},
\end{equation}
the least conservative point on the path at which rollout $i$ stays silent, and $V_i = \infty$
if none is: a success that lasts the whole limit ($\rho_i \ge 1$) alarms at every point.
Because the path is monotone, rollout $i$ is silent at $\lambda$ exactly when
$\lambda \ge V_i$.\footnote{$\lambda$ takes the finitely many values at which the timer (one
inference) or the score changes the alarm, so the minimum is attained.}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:fpr}
Let $n$ calibration successes and a new successful rollout be exchangeable, and let the path
$\gamma$ not depend on the calibration successes. Set $\hat\lambda = V_{(r)}$, the $r$-th smallest
$V_i$ with $r = \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil$, or $\hat\lambda = \infty$ if $r > n$. If
$\hat\lambda = \infty$, the calibration rule cannot certify a usable point at the target and the
monitor is disabled: it raises no alarm. Otherwise it runs at $\gamma(\hat\lambda)$. Then, over
calibration draws, $\Pr(\text{monitor enabled and false alarm on the new success}) \le \alpha$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{IEEEproof}
An enabled monitor alarms on the new success if and only if $V_{n+1} > \hat\lambda$, and a
disabled one ($\hat\lambda = \infty$) never does, so the event is $\{V_{n+1} > V_{(r)}\}$ with
$V_{(r)} = \infty$ for $r > n$. By exchangeability, the rank of $V_{n+1}$ among
$V_1, \dots, V_{n+1}$ is uniform (ties broken at random), so $\Pr(V_{n+1} > V_{(r)}) \le
1 - r/(n+1) \le \alpha$: the split-conformal quantile lemma~\cite{angelopoulos2021gentle}.
\end{IEEEproof}

The path may use any data except calibration successes, such as development rollouts or
calibration \emph{failures}. The guarantee is per trajectory and marginal over tasks and
calibration draws, not a cap on a finite test set or on each task (on the test set, one task
had 4 of 10 successes alarmed); a per-task version needs 19 calibration successes per task.

\textbf{Adaptive and fixed paths.} Our Stage~1 results use \emph{adaptive-path calibration}:
every detector fused with P uses the path $\delta = \lambda$, $\tau_s = u + s\lambda$, with
$(u, s)$ the grid point of largest mean lead on calibration failures, each point placed at its own
$\hat\lambda$. This choice also uses the calibration successes, so Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr}
bounds each candidate path, not the chosen one; over 200 re-splits of the successes, the choice
raised the expected test FPR from 0.047 (P) to about 0.055. A \emph{fixed path}, chosen
independently of the calibration successes, restores the bound: chosen on the validation set,
Hide-and-Seek + P still gains 0.072 at 0.061 FPR (SAFE + P: 0.007), although the gain remains
uncertain. Stage~2 uses fixed paths
only (Sec.~\ref{sec:plan}).

\section{Hypotheses}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

\textbf{Early warning (H1).} At a 5\% calibration target, OR-fusing a policy-internal score
with the per-task timer P increases the mean warning lead over P alone, also when P is loosened to
the fused detector's realized false alarms (matched FPR).

\textbf{Policy evidence (H2).} At matched FPR, policy scores fused with P lead more than scores
without rollout-specific information: random noise, or features or scores resampled from other
rollouts of the task.

\textbf{Second policy (H3).} On a second VLA, RLDX-1~\cite{kim2026rldx}, SAFE + P and
Hide-and-Seek + P also lead P, including at matched false alarms.

\textbf{Value of earlier warning (H4).} Within a budget of $1.25\,H_k$, resetting and retrying
when Hide-and-Seek + P alarms yields a higher success rate than resetting when P alarms.

Sec.~\ref{sec:results} tests H1 and H2; Stage~2 addresses H3 and H4 (Sec.~\ref{sec:plan}).

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Policy and tasks.} We use the released Xiaomi-Robotics-1 (XR-1) RoboCasa365
checkpoint~\cite{xiaomi2026robotics} on the 38 official RoboCasa365
tasks~\cite{nasiriany2024robocasa,robocasa365} whose published success lies between 5 and 45 of
50 (10 atomic and 28 composite tasks).

\textbf{Data.} Disjoint sets of rollouts serve one purpose each. A \emph{training set} (1,824
rollouts) fits every learned detector and P's $\rho_k$. A \emph{validation set} (380 successes
and 380 failures, 10 per class per task), with its own calibration set (3 successes and 3
failures per task), was used to develop the timers and the fused detectors. A \emph{test set} of the same size,
with new seeds, and a new calibration set of 380 successes and 380 failures served the tests of
H1 and H2.

\textbf{Unseen tasks.} Each task is held out once (4 folds): monitors are retrained and
thresholds calibrated without it, with OOD references and score normalization pooled over the
other tasks. P needs the task's own successes, so the comparator is G. (Hide-and-Seek's
evaluation~\cite{park2026hideseek} uses random 7/3 task splits instead.)

\textbf{Baselines.} We compare three families (Table~\ref{tab:raw}).
\emph{OOD detectors}, fitted on successful rollouts only: Mahalanobis
distance~\cite{lee2018mahalanobis}, cosine k-NN~\cite{sun2022knn},
PCA-KMeans~\cite{liu2024siriusfleet} and RND~\cite{he2024rediffuser}. \emph{Failure
classifiers}, trained with rollout labels: SAFE-MLP~\cite{gu2025safe},
ActProbe~\cite{huang2026actprobe} and Hide-and-Seek~\cite{park2026hideseek}. \emph{Elapsed
time}, with no policy access: G and P. Token-uncertainty baselines need discrete action tokens
and STAC~\cite{agia2024sentinel} overlapping chunks, which XR-1 does not produce.

\textbf{Metrics.} Following~\cite{romer2025fiper,park2026hideseek}, at thresholds calibrated for
FPR $\le 0.05$ we report test FPR, balanced accuracy (bACC; equal to weighted accuracy on our
balanced sets), time-weighted accuracy $\mathrm{TWA} = \frac{1}{2}(\bar\ell + 1 -
\mathrm{FPR})$ and detection time $\mathrm{DT} = 1 - \bar\ell$, a miss counting as 1. As
in~\cite{park2026hideseek}, each learned detector is trained with 3 random seeds, and tables give
the mean $\pm$ std of single models across these training seeds. For H1 and H2 we use
\emph{three-seed ensembles}, which average the 3 models' scores. \emph{Matched} gains compare
with P loosened on the test set to the fused detector's false alarms, which favors the timer. For
the two central matched gains, 95\% bootstrap CIs resample the test set's tasks, then failures
within each task (2,000 resamples, paired across methods). They are conditional on the fitted
thresholds and the matched timer, so they exclude calibration and matching uncertainty.

\subsection{Standalone failure detection}

Evaluated on their own, as in prior work (Table~\ref{tab:raw}), the OOD detectors and failure
classifiers catch 36\% to 69\% of the failures on seen tasks at 5\% FPR. The two timers, which
never read the policy, catch all of them (bACC 0.97 and 0.99, against at most 0.82). The global
timer alarms last, but P (DT 0.775) alarms earlier than every learned detector except
Hide-and-Seek (0.754), which alone exceeds its TWA. On unseen tasks, Hide-and-Seek is the only
detector above the global timer's TWA.

\subsection{Warning lead over the per-task timer}

\textbf{SAFE: a small gain (H1, H2).} On the test set, the SAFE ensemble fused with P leads P by
0.030 of the time limit at 0.061 FPR. At the same target, random and within-task resampled
scores gain at most 0.009, so the gain is not an artifact of the fusion itself. At matched false alarms, however,
it shrinks to 0.015 (95\% CI [$-0.007$, 0.041]).

\textbf{Hide-and-Seek: an earlier warning (H1, H2).} The Hide-and-Seek ensemble fused with P
leads P by 0.071 at the same 0.061 FPR (random and resampled scores: at most 0.012). At matched
false alarms it gains 0.056 (95\% CI [$-0.014$, 0.120]), and its point estimates are positive at
every target from 2\% to 10\%, where SAFE's average 0.013 (Fig.~\ref{fig:pertask}b). Its
advantage over SAFE + P (0.041) is uncertain. A single Hide-and-Seek model gains only $0.039 \pm
0.006$ (Table~\ref{tab:lead}), so the ensemble is part of its warning.

\textbf{Where the gain comes from.} The gain is uneven across tasks (Fig.~\ref{fig:pertask}a).
All six policy scores gain more on the 10 atomic tasks (0.045 to 0.160) than on the 28
composite ones (0.008 to 0.039). Hide-and-Seek + P alarms earlier than P on 21 tasks and later on
16, where its looser timer margin spends the false-alarm budget on the score; five tasks carry
78\% of its gain, and SAFE helps on the same tasks (rank correlation 0.71).

\subsection{Is the extra warning learned elapsed time?}

A recurrent detector trained on rollouts that all fail at the limit could learn task durations,
and the zero-input ablation does separate failures as well as Hide-and-Seek (Sec.~I). On the
test set, however, the first-step, zero-input and shuffle ablations gain $-0.003$, $-0.003$ and
$-0.011$ over P at matched false alarms, against 0.056 for Hide-and-Seek (and 0.070, 0.059 and
0.060 less than it at the same target): its warning develops during the rollout and is neither
elapsed time nor the task's typical trajectory.

\subsection{When do failures become irreversible?}

One annotator, blind to alarms, marked the point of no return (the first state from which the
task could not be completed without outside help) on 48 test-set failures: 38 (79\%) reached no
such state before the limit (\emph{recoverable}), most often because the robot was slow or
nearly succeeded; a second annotator agreed on 11 of 12 ($\kappa = 0.80$). On all 10 failures that did become
irreversible, P alarmed after the point of no return, and no detector flags more than 8\% of
failures by half the limit.

\textbf{Second policy (E1).} We test H3 on new RLDX-1 rollouts: fresh calibration and test sets
of 20 successes and 20 failures for each of its 20 tasks (about 2,500 rollouts). The detectors
(SAFE-LSTM, Hide-and-Seek), $H_k$, $\rho_k$ and each fused path are fixed on the existing
rollouts, so the new calibration successes set only $\hat\lambda$ and
Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr} applies to the evaluated detector. We compare each with P, at the
same target and at matched false alarms, and with random-score fusions.

\textbf{Reset and retry (E2).} We evaluate H4 in closed loop on 1,000 RLDX-1 scenes (50 per
task) with a shared budget of $1.25\,H_k$. Each scene runs once without reset; for each monitor,
we branch from this run at its first alarm, restore the scene and retry in the remaining budget.
Monitor inference latency is charged against this budget, and both monitors use identical reset
and retry accounting. We report the paired success-rate difference between Hide-and-Seek + P and
P resets on the same scenes, with a 95\% CI, together with unnecessary resets and wasted time.

\textbf{Further analyses (E3).} Action-only and multi-sample monitors, richer time baselines,
unseen tasks on new rollouts, and lead from the point of no return (250 to 500 failures).

\section{Conclusion and Limitations}

When failure means running out of time, a monitor must beat a timer at the same false-alarm
rate. Against a per-task timer at the same target FPR, three-seed ensembles of SAFE and
Hide-and-Seek added 0.030 and 0.071 of the time limit on new test rollouts; at matched false
alarms these shrink to 0.015 and 0.056, and both remain uncertain. Input ablations trace
Hide-and-Seek's warning to the rollout, and on XR-1 it carries over to unseen tasks, where
SAFE's does not. Stage~2 tests both monitors on a second policy and measures their value in
closed-loop retry.

\textbf{Limitations.} Alarms were recorded, not acted on, in simulation, and lead is measured to
the time limit, not to an irreversible failure. RLDX-1 is evaluated only on recorded rollouts.
The timers and fusions were designed on the validation set, and the matched, per-task,
unseen-task and FIPER analyses were added after the test set was scored;
Hide-and-Seek, the better of two supervised baselines on the validation set, was tested on a
test set already used for SAFE. Monitors assume a known task and time limit; fixed-horizon
data need failure-time labels. Only 10 annotated failures became irreversible.
