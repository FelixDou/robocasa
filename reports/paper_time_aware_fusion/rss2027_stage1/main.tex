\title{Time Is the Baseline to Beat:\\Lead Time over a Timer as the Test for VLA Failure Monitors}

\author{Author Names Omitted for Anonymous Review. Paper-ID [add your ID here]}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
On RoboCasa365, a failed vision-language-action (VLA) rollout is one that reaches its time
limit. A runtime failure monitor therefore competes with a timer that alarms at a fixed
fraction of that limit. Calibrated at a 5\% false-positive rate (FPR), such a timer flags
every one of 380 prospective failures, about 12 policy inferences before the timeout. Recall
cannot separate a learned monitor from this timer; only lead time can. We state this as a
testable hypothesis: at a matched calibrated FPR, alarming when \emph{either} a
policy-internal score or the timer crosses its threshold warns earlier than the timer and
than any combination of time signals alone. We give a split-conformal calibration of this
two-threshold rule with a finite-sample FPR guarantee. Exploratory evidence on 760
prospective rollouts of one VLA on 38 tasks favours time. Fusing the SAFE failure score with a
single timer raises the mean warning lead from 0.090 to 0.207 of the time limit (paired gain
0.117, 95\% CI 0.060--0.178), and noise fused with that timer reaches only 0.101. But a
per-task timer, which alarms past the 95th percentile of each task's development success
durations plus a conformally calibrated margin, reaches 0.195 at 0.045 FPR without reading the
policy, and adding SAFE to it gains only 0.007 (CI $-0.003$ to $0.019$). Stage~2 tests the hypothesis
against this stronger time baseline on a preregistered fresh cohort and a second VLA, and asks whether earlier alarms raise task success within a fixed
time budget when they trigger a reset and retry.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Runtime monitors for VLA policies read the policy's hidden state and predict eventual
failure~\cite{gu2025safe}, or score how unusual that state is relative to successful
rollouts~\cite{xu2025faildetect,romer2025fiper}. They are compared with each other, but not
with what the robot knows for free: how long the attempt has been running.

In RoboCasa365, as in most simulated manipulation benchmarks, an episode ends at first
success or at a time limit, and in our test cohort every one of 380 failed rollouts ran to
its limit. A timer at 90\% of the limit therefore flags all of them at 4.7\% FPR. Against it,
a monitor can add only one thing: an earlier alarm at the same false-alarm rate, which leaves
time to stop, reset or ask for help. We make this lead time the test, with three
contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A testable hypothesis and endpoint.} We define the warning lead of an alarm
  as the fraction of the time limit left when it fires (zero for a missed failure) and state
  four hypotheses with decision rules fixed before Stage~2 data are collected
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:hypotheses}).
  \item \textbf{Calibrated OR-fusion with a guarantee.} We alarm when either a
  policy-internal score or the timer crosses its threshold, and calibrate both thresholds
  along a nested path with a split-conformal rule that bounds the FPR on a new successful
  rollout by $\alpha$ in finite samples (Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr}).
  \item \textbf{Preliminary evidence under a strict protocol.} With thresholds frozen on a
  separate calibration cohort and evaluated once on a disjoint prospective cohort, SAFE
  fused with a single timer more than doubles its mean lead, but a per-task timer that never
  reads the policy matches it, and SAFE adds only 0.007 on top (Sec.~\ref{sec:evidence}).
\end{enumerate}
The preliminary data point to no useful effect. A negative confirmatory result would still be
informative: on timeout-defined benchmarks, policy internals would add little to duration
statistics, and monitors should be evaluated on failures that end early.

\section{Problem Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

\textbf{Time.} We measure time in four units (Table~\ref{tab:time}). The simulator advances
in \emph{control steps} at 20\,Hz, RoboCasa's default control rate, which our collection
code does not change. The policy is queried once per 16-step action chunk and executes the
whole chunk, so one \emph{policy inference} covers 16 control steps, or 0.8\,s. Inferences
are our clock: $t = 1, 2, \dots$ counts them, and a monitor can change its decision only
at an inference. The official time limit of task $k$ is $T_k$ control steps; in
inferences it is $H_k = \lceil T_k/16 \rceil$, between 29 and 272 for our tasks (22.5 to
217.5\,s). Videos keep every second control step (10\,Hz) and play at 20 frames per second,
twice real time. We use them only for figures. Every fraction of time in this paper, such
as $t/H_k$, the timer fraction $c$ and the lead $\ell$, is a fraction of $H_k$.

\textbf{Monitoring.} A monitor watches a rollout of a known task $k$ and decides at each
inference $t$ whether to alarm, using only the policy features so far, $t$, the task and
$H_k$. The first crossing is the alarm $T$ (in inferences). FPR is the fraction of successful
rollouts with any alarm. A rollout of length $L$ inferences ends at success or at $H_k$; for
a failure ($L = H_k$) the \emph{warning lead} is $\ell = (L - T)/H_k$, or 0 if missed, and an
alarm at the final inference counts with $\ell = 0$. The mean lead $\bar\ell$ combines recall
and earliness. Detectors are compared at operating points fixed on a calibration cohort under
FPR $\le \alpha = 0.05$. Confidence intervals come from a paired bootstrap that resamples
tasks, then rollouts within each task (2,000 replicates).

\section{Hypotheses}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

We test four hypotheses. H1 and H2 are evaluated on a fresh confirmatory cohort with the
decision rules below, fixed before that cohort is opened; H3 and H4 are the Stage~2
extensions (Sec.~\ref{sec:plan}).

\textbf{H1 (lead time, primary).} At thresholds calibrated for FPR $\le 0.05$, OR-fusing a
policy-internal score with the per-task timer (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}), the strongest time-only
monitor we know, increases the mean warning lead over that timer alone.
\emph{Supported} if the paired bootstrap 95\% CI of $\Delta\bar\ell$ lies above zero, the
point gain is at least $0.05$ of the time limit, and the fused test FPR is at most $0.075$
with a Wilson 95\% upper bound at most $0.10$. As a secondary check, the fused detector must
also beat a timer whose fraction is tuned \emph{on the test cohort} to the fused detector's
test FPR, which favours the timer.

\textbf{H2 (the gain needs policy evidence).} Fusions that contain no policy-internal
evidence (Gaussian noise, or SAFE scores resampled within task, each fused with the per-task
timer) gain less than $0.05$ of the time limit over the per-task timer.

\textbf{H3 (generality).} H1 holds for a second VLA with a different architecture.

\textbf{H4 (utility).} When an alarm triggers a reset and retry, the OR-fused monitor yields
a higher task success rate than the timer within the same per-task time budget.

Only H1 and H2 are confirmatory; every other interval in this paper is descriptive. On the
opened data the H1 gain is 0.007, so we expect H1 to fail; that outcome would show that, when
failure means timeout, a policy's hidden state adds no usable warning over task duration.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}


\subsection{Signals}

\textbf{Policy score.} At each inference we take the final-layer action-token hidden state
of the policy's action head (1,024 dimensions). The SAFE MLP~\cite{gu2025safe} maps it to a
failure score $u^{(j)}_t$ for each of three training seeds $j$. With per-task location
$m^{(j)}_k$ and scale $\sigma^{(j)}_k$ estimated on development rollouts, the policy score is
\begin{equation}
  s_t = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{j=1}^{3} \frac{\max_{t' \le t} u^{(j)}_{t'} - m^{(j)}_k}{\sigma^{(j)}_k}.
\end{equation}
The running maximum makes $s_t$ monotone and does not change when a threshold is first
crossed. Success-only alternatives (Mahalanobis~\cite{lee2018mahalanobis},
kNN~\cite{sun2022knn}, PCA-KMeans and RND~\cite{burda2019rnd}) are fitted on successful
development rollouts and aggregated the same way.

\textbf{Timers.} The global timer alarms once $t \ge c\,H_k$ for a fraction $c \in (0, 1]$
shared by all tasks; it uses only the task's time limit. The per-task timer uses
$c_k = \min(1, \rho_k + \delta)$, where $\rho_k$ is the 95th percentile of task $k$'s
development success durations as fractions of $H_k$ and the shared margin $\delta$ is set by
Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr} on calibration successes. It needs successful rollouts of each
task, but no failures and no policy features. We fixed the 95th percentile after auditing the
opened cohort, so we report 0.75, 0.90 and 1.0 (the longest success) as sensitivity.

\textbf{Time risk (baseline).} The survival-style alternative estimates
$r_t = \Pr(\text{failure} \mid k, \text{still running at } t)$ from development rollouts: with
$n_t$ of them still running at $t$ and $f_t$ of those failing, it fits an increasing
isotonic regression in $t/H_k$ to $(f_t + 0.5)/(n_t + 1)$, weighted by $n_t$. Because every
failure runs to $H_k$, $r_t$ is a task-specific timer; its smoothing keeps it below a high
threshold in tasks with few development failures, which is why it misses failures the
plain timer catches.

\subsection{OR-fusion}

The fused monitor alarms at
\begin{equation}
  T = \min\{\, t : s_t > \tau_s \ \text{or}\ t \ge c\,H_k \,\}.
\end{equation}
Neither signal is restricted in time: the policy score may alarm early, and the timer
guarantees an alarm on every failure, at $c\,H_k$ at the latest. The lead gain over the
timer comes only from failures on which the policy score crosses first.

\subsection{Calibration with a finite-sample FPR guarantee}
\label{sec:guarantee}

A successful rollout $i$ of length $L_i$ (in inferences) raises a false alarm under
$(\tau_s, c)$ if and only if $M_i = \max_{t \le L_i} s_t > \tau_s$ or $\rho_i = L_i/H_{k_i}
\ge c$, so its false alarm depends only on the pair $(M_i, \rho_i)$. Fix in advance a path
$\gamma(\lambda) = (\tau_s(\lambda), c(\lambda))$, $\lambda \in [0, 1]$, with both
coordinates continuous and nondecreasing, so that a larger $\lambda$ is more conservative and
$\gamma(1)$ never alarms on a success. For each calibration success define
\begin{equation}
  V_i = \min\{\lambda : M_i \le \tau_s(\lambda) \ \text{and}\ \rho_i < c(\lambda)\},
\end{equation}
the least conservative point on the path at which rollout $i$ stays silent. Because the path
is monotone, rollout $i$ is silent at $\lambda$ exactly when $\lambda \ge V_i$.\footnote{The
strict $\rho_i < c(\lambda)$ makes the set right-open; taking $c$ on a finite grid, as the
timer's resolution of one inference does anyway, makes the minimum attained.}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:fpr}
Let $n$ calibration successes and a new successful rollout be exchangeable, and let the path
$\gamma$ not depend on the calibration successes. Set $\hat\lambda = V_{(\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)
\rceil)}$, the $\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil$-th smallest $V_i$ (or $\hat\lambda = 1$ if
that rank exceeds $n$). Then the OR-fused monitor at $\gamma(\hat\lambda)$ raises a false
alarm on the new success with probability at most $\alpha$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{IEEEproof}
The new rollout alarms at $\hat\lambda$ if and only if $V_{n+1} > \hat\lambda = V_{(r)}$ with
$r = \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil$. By exchangeability, the rank of $V_{n+1}$ among
$V_1, \dots, V_{n+1}$ is uniform on $\{1, \dots, n+1\}$ (ties broken at random), so
$\Pr(V_{n+1} > V_{(r)}) \le 1 - r/(n+1) \le \alpha$; this is the split-conformal quantile
lemma~\cite{angelopoulos2021gentle} applied to the scalar $V$.
\end{IEEEproof}

The path may use any data except calibration successes, such as development rollouts or
calibration \emph{failures}. The guarantee is marginal over tasks; a per-task version needs
19 calibration successes per task. The deployed FPR is Beta-distributed given calibration: it
exceeds 0.075 with probability 0.065 for $n = 114$, but 0.021 for $n = 380$.

The preliminary results below use the simpler joint search of the original protocol: among
all pairs of observed calibration maxima with calibration FPR $\le \alpha$, keep the pair with
the largest mean lead (highest recall for the time-risk fusions). Selecting the best of many
non-nested pairs on the same 114 successes has no finite-sample guarantee and is optimistic,
which matches the test FPRs of 0.058--0.068 we observe for two-threshold detectors against
0.044 on calibration. The per-task timer rows already use Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr}, with the
path $\delta = \lambda$, $\tau_s = u + s\lambda$ and $(u, s)$ chosen on calibration failures;
Stage~2 uses it for every detector.

\textbf{The timer is the baseline to beat.} The timer ($c = 0.903$) alarms on all 380 test
failures at 0.047 FPR with a fixed lead of 0.090 (Table~\ref{tab:main}). The time-risk curve
misses 140 failures, all in 14 tasks whose smoothed curve never reaches its threshold, so recall
gains measured against it (to 0.90--0.96 for SAFE and four success-only scores) vanish here.

\textbf{SAFE adds lead over a single timer.} SAFE + timer raises the mean lead to 0.207 (paired
gain 0.117, CI 0.060--0.178; 20.2 against 11.7 inferences) and flags 42\% of failures by
$0.75\,H_k$ (Figs.~\ref{fig:qualitative} and~\ref{fig:lead}), at 0.058 test FPR. Noise fused
with the timer reaches 0.101, and the time risk with the timer 0.125. Among rollouts still
running at 10, 25, 50 and 75\% of the limit, SAFE's within-task ROC-AUC is 0.55--0.60, against
0.41--0.51 for noise. Bootstrapping calibration as well, with thresholds re-selected, keeps the
gain positive (0.125, CI 0.031--0.246) but lets the fused test FPR reach 0.16, so threshold
uncertainty dominates. On test-cohort frontiers, OR and AND fusion reach the same lead at 5\%
FPR (0.208 and 0.211); OR keeps the timer's guaranteed alarm.

\textbf{A per-task timer removes the gain (H1, exploratory).} The per-task timer reaches
$\bar\ell = 0.195$ at 0.045 FPR without reading the policy. SAFE + timer leads it by an
insignificant 0.012 (CI $-0.044$ to $0.063$), and by $-0.003$ against a per-task timer tuned on
test to the same FPR. SAFE + per-task timer reaches 0.202, a gain of 0.007 (CI $-0.003$ to
$0.019$), far below the 0.05 margin of H1. The two effects cancel across tasks:
in the 14 tasks where the time risk never fires, SAFE + timer's mean lead is 0.405 against
0.221 for the per-task timer; in the other 24 it is 0.092 against 0.180.
The percentile matters: with the longest development success ($q = 1$) the per-task timer
reaches only 0.125 and SAFE adds 0.057 (CI $-0.003$ to $0.119$); with $q = 0.90$, 0.169 and
0.028 (CI $-0.012$ to $0.067$).

\textbf{No early warning yet.} No detector flags more than 8\% of failures by half of the
limit; whether a late warning is usable is H4.

\section{Stage 2 Plan}
\label{sec:plan}

\textbf{E1: Confirmatory test of H1 and H2.} A fresh cohort of 760 rollouts (10 per class per
task, new environment seeds) is being collected and has not been opened. Before opening it,
we amend its preregistration with the per-task timer (95th percentile of development
successes, margin by Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr}) as the primary comparator and $\Delta\bar\ell$ as
the primary endpoint, with the H1 and H2 decision rules of Sec.~\ref{sec:hypotheses}. The
earlier recall endpoint against the time risk becomes secondary. We enlarge calibration to
10 successes per task ($n = 380$), apply Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr} with a path chosen on
development data, and bootstrap the calibration cohort as well as the test cohort,
re-selecting thresholds in each replicate, so the intervals include threshold
uncertainty.\pending{merge the amendment (PR \#3) and name the calibration seed range before opening the confirmatory cohort.}

\textbf{E2: Second policy (H3).} We repeat the timer, time-risk, SAFE and fused rows with
$\pi_0$ or RLDX-1 on the same 38 tasks, reusing saved rollouts where hidden states were
recorded. A policy with overlapping chunks also lets
us run chunk-consistency monitors~\cite{agia2024sentinel,huang2026actprobe}.

\textbf{E3: Closed-loop reset and retry (H4).} On 8 to 10 tasks with mid-range success, each
episode gets a budget of $2H_k$ inferences. Under each monitor (none, timer, SAFE + timer),
an alarm ends the attempt, resets the scene to its initial state and starts a new attempt
while budget remains; without a monitor, the retry starts at the timeout. We compare success
within the budget, paired by environment seed, and count successes aborted by false alarms.
The gain is bounded by the failure rate times the retry
success rate times the fraction of failures whose extra lead leaves room for a retry; a null
result would bound the value of any such monitor on this benchmark.

\textbf{E4: Stronger baselines and checks.} (i) Action-only monitors that need no hidden state:
chunk magnitude and the disagreement of a second flow sample. (ii) Richer time baselines: a
stall detector on end-effector motion and a discrete-time hazard model on simple
observables, so that ``time'' means the best signal available without the policy's
internals. (iii) OR- and AND-fusion compared on full recall--FPR curves rather than single
points. (iv) SAFE faithfulness checks and precision at deployment
prevalence. (v) An unseen-task split, holding
tasks out of SAFE training and normalization; the timer needs only $H_k$ and transfers as is.

\textbf{E5: Failures that end early.} Every failure here ends at the limit. We will label,
on a subset, when a failure became unrecoverable (a dropped or displaced object), and report
lead relative to that moment, since these are the failures a deployed robot most needs to
catch early.

\section{Limitations}

The preliminary results come from one policy in simulation, on a cohort opened before the
fused detectors and the timer were defined; only E1 can make them confirmatory. The
two-threshold detectors exceed the FPR budget on test, which Proposition~\ref{prop:fpr} and
a larger calibration cohort address. The timer, the time risk and the per-task normalization
assume that the task and its time limit are known, and the policy score needs white-box
access to the policy and development rollouts of each task; on our data, that access bought 0.007 of lead over the per-task timer. Test cohorts are balanced by
quota, so recall and FPR are per class rather than at deployment prevalence.

\section{Conclusion}

When failure means timeout, a calibrated timer catches every failure, and the question for a
learned monitor becomes how much earlier it can warn at the same false-alarm rate. On one VLA
and 38 tasks, fusing a hidden-state failure score with a single timer more than doubled the
mean warning lead, but a per-task timer built from development success durations matched
it without reading the policy, and the hidden state added 0.007 on top of it. Stage~2 tests this against the
per-task timer on a fresh cohort and a second policy, with a finite-sample FPR guarantee, and asks the question that decides
its value: whether the extra warning lets a robot recover more often within the same time.
